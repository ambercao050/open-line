I WANTED THAT UNNATURAL FEELING

An Interview with Bian Tianlei

Do your works usually begin with a visual image or an emotion?

Bian: Yes. In most cases, I think it actually comes from a visual element. Although saying that sounds so commercial, so lacking in artistic spirit — I don't know. For me, whether I'm painting or making animation, the first idea is always that a picture appears in my head, and I want to express that picture — whether in a single painting or in a whole sequence. For example, with animation, the reason I make the animation is just that I want to show this one scene. I make a whole film only for the sake of that scene. But later, during the creative process, I gradually project the feelings from my own life into it. So the finished work also ends up carrying emotion.

“I make a whole film only for the sake of that scene.”

How did you find your own visual language? Are there particular elements that feel distinctly yours?

Bian: Right now, I think fire is an element I especially want to express. For one thing, I think my style of painting fire is fairly distinctive. For another, flame can express a lot: unease, instability, irritability — negative emotions can all be expressed in fire. And if you handle the overall atmosphere of the picture well, it can also express positive emotions. It can express so much that I put some fire in almost every painting. But I think this is also something I need to improve. I can't always rely on this one element to express emotion. Apart from that, I think my painting style as a whole is mine; I don't feel there's anything of anyone else's in it. The Bosch elements are something I deliberately wanted to learn from. In my undergraduate years, during the year I took a leave of absence, I came across a lot of material related to mysticism and medieval Christian aesthetics. In it there was a great deal of Bosch, and further on some Impressionism — Symbolist paintings and so on. I really loved that they could express so much implicit emotion — their thinking — within one painting, in a very abstract way. I wanted that.

How does the camera in Dream Is a Dream reflect your fear of being watched?

Bian: The camera represents more of an objective gaze — there's not much subjectivity in it. It stands for the sense that, in your world, in some corner you haven't noticed, someone is looking at you, or something is watching you. Like the surveillance cameras in our lives. Or when we're out walking, there are always people who look you up and down. That's something I've always been afraid of in my heart: being stared at, being watched. I wanted to throw that inescapable feeling of being watched into the animation. And there's another layer: wherever there's a camera, you know it isn't the real world. Because the story of Dream Is a Dream is about a protagonist who always feels he is not in the real world. He believes the real world is better, and he keeps trying to find the real world. In the end he discovers that the whole thing is an animation, a film — that there's nothing real, and even he himself doesn't exist. He's a character. At the end, once he knows that he himself isn't real, he chooses to end his own life. So the camera represents an unreal gaze. Edited from 11:34–12:07. Why did you choose animation as a medium for expressing these experiences, rather than painting alone?

Bian: Because I chose animation. I'm doing an animation graduation project. [Laughs.] Let me think of something more highfalutin. Because animation is something that contains flowing time. It has a length; it isn't a single frame. You have to stare at it for a long time, and try to dig many, many emotions out of one image. Once there's a duration, within that context you can express countless things. And you can also give the audience a sense of immersion. I've always felt — even now when I paint — that animation is a kind of transposed experience. I got this from an interview with Jodorowsky. Setting character animation aside, what we do — experimental animation or experimental film — is really a kind of shamanic ritual for summoning spirits. The original recording has a brief gap here. Bian continues by describing ritual and immersion. Bian: Animation is the same. When we make a short film, we don't only have to make the audience feel visually detached from reality — we also have to do it in the music and the sound design, so they strongly feel this is not reality. And yet you make them willing to accept this non-real environment. That's the feeling I want to express.

How did your experience in California influence Like a Vision, Like a Nightmare?

Bian: The original inspiration for this painting came from when we went to Santa Monica. On the way back there were fish-scale clouds in the sky, strip after strip. And the vegetation on the California hills is also in clumps — round, ball-like clusters. Those round clumps of vegetation and the round clouds in the sky gave the whole view a kind of pattern. So I thought that while painting this, I could try to make the clouds and the trees form a regular pattern, as if it were printed across the entire picture. It would look as though the sky and the mountains were unnatural. I wanted that unnatural feeling. The elements in this painting are basically images that struck me hard when I first arrived. For example, this little shopping cart and the goods in it. Those aren't things that happened to me while I was shopping. This is the homeless — the homeless people on the street. I feel they're pitiable. This country is actually a very wealthy country, and yet the public services don't seem to take very good care of these people wandering the streets. And for me, painting it on fire isn't only about expressing a kind of incomprehension or sympathy for the homeless. More than that, I think that when the two of us first arrived, our moods were also very bad, so maybe we have a bit of that state in us too. My first idea for the title was "Like a Vision, Like a Dream." But then I felt that putting "California" and "Like a Dream" together would make it sound as if my dream had finally come true — that I'd arrived at the place I'd always dreamed of. It's completely not like that. I have no feelings for California. I wanted to erase that happy emotion, so I replaced "Dream" with "Nightmare."

How does your creative process work, and how important is it for viewers to understand your intentions?

Bian: It starts like this: in daily life — when I'm washing dishes, mopping the floor, or maybe painting something else — my brain is in a half-relaxed state, and it's like dreaming. Lots of things I've seen in the past flash through my head, along with things fused together like chimeras. Then suddenly I feel an emotion that wants to put this thing into my painting. During the painting process — say I do a rough draft first. I think of this person's pose, and I want to paint this pose. What the pose expresses, I haven't thought about at the start. I paint the pose first. Once it's down, I use rational thinking to adjust the structure of the whole picture — because in the end the painting has to look good, so I use some rational thought to recompose. But anyway, when I paint I rarely find reasons for myself. I paint whatever I think of. Explaining it in the end might get troublesome. If you want to explain it, you have to work out how to phrase what it expresses — that's exhausting. I think with an artwork, if in the end you break it down until it's completely logical, then it loses that mystique of art. Or the work even goes beyond what you meant to express. I'd prefer it if viewers didn't ask me — didn't ask the author — "What does this express?" Instead: as a viewer, when I first see this painting, what does it give me? Maybe what they think it expresses isn't what I had in mind, but I think that's all okay. Whether it's painting, or film — in the end, it's acceptable for what the viewer experiences to be completely different from what the author wanted to express.

“If in the end you break it down until it's completely logical, then it loses that mystique of art.”
